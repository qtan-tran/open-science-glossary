The long-running structural conflict in which the subscription prices of scholarly journals rise faster than library budgets, forcing libraries to cancel titles while publishers' profits remain high. The term has been used in librarianship since the late 1980s; the "Big Deal" is the publishing response of the late 1990s, licensing a publisher's entire electronic journal portfolio at a discount to subscribing per title.

## Details

- First-wave new entry (D-5), maintainer-reviewed on 2026-11-20
- The Big Deal shifted spending from à la carte subscriptions to bundled licences, concentrating budgets with the largest publishers and raising the barrier for new competitors
- Consequences visible across this glossary: journal cancellations, ever more of the budget concentrated on ever fewer large bundles, and cost pressure that motivated transformative agreements, Plan S, and the Diamond OA resurgence
- Monopoly economics: commercial publishers profit from unpaid peer review and editorial labour while selling the result back to institutions

> [!WARNING]
> The "crisis" is not crisis-pricing alone but a locked-in system: switching costs and journal prestige hierarchies keep institutions paying even when price per utility falls
