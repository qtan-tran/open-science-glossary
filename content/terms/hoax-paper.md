A hoax paper is a deliberately fake or parodic scholarly article submitted, and sometimes published, to test or expose weaknesses in peer review and editorial processes. Famous cases include physicist Alan Sokal's 1996 "Transgressing the Boundaries" article in Social Text, the 2017 "Conceptual Penis as a Social Construct" article in Cogent Social Sciences (whose authors, Boghossian and Lindsay, revealed it as a prank), and the 2018 "grievance studies" project, in which twenty deliberately absurd papers were submitted to journals in gender and identity studies.

## Details

- **Findings:** Sokal showed that fashionable jargon could pass a leading cultural-studies journal without statistical or empirical review; the grievance studies project, in turn, saw seven papers accepted (one later retracted en masse), which its authors presented as evidence of ideological bias and low review standards.
- **Limits of the evidence:** Hoax papers test vulnerable venues under adversarial conditions; their results cannot demonstrate that peer review fails in general, only that it failed in those cases.
- **Aftermath:** The Cogent Social Sciences case prompted the publisher and editor to publish a detailed post-mortem and retraction, a rare case in which the hoax resulted in visible institutional reckoning rather than rhetoric alone.

> [!WARNING]
> Hoax authors themselves face ethics scrutiny: the grievance studies authors were investigated for IRB violations, so the method is contested from both sides (review quality and research-ethics grounds) and should be cited carefully as evidence of anything.
