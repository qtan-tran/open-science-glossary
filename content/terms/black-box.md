Bruno Latour's metaphor for a technical or scientific construct that has become so routinely accepted that nobody examines how it works inside: its history of construction, controversy and maintenance disappears from view, and it is treated simply as an input that produces outputs. Airplanes, statistics packages, journal metrics and established methods can all function as black boxes in scholarly life.

## Details

- Second-wave new entry (approved 2026-11-20): draft awaiting maintainer review
- Black boxes are not inherently bad: they are how any complex system becomes usable day to day; the problem arises when failures or bias hide inside a box nobody is allowed or able to open
- Opening happens in moments of controversy: when a result fails to replicate, an algorithm is audited, or an infrastructure's governance is challenged, the box is temporarily reopened and its inner workings become visible again
- Latour's point is symmetrical: science and technology studies follow the construction work before closure, and see only black boxes after it

> [!WARNING]
> Treating a tool or indicator as a black box is a practical necessity, but citing it as authority is not: replications, audits and documentation requests exist precisely to keep important boxes re-openable
