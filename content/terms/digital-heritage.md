Digital heritage is the part of cultural heritage that is either born digital (websites, datasets, software, born-digital documents and art) or turned into digital form through digitization, and that is judged worth preserving for future generations. UNESCO's 2003 Charter on the Preservation of the Digital Heritage framed it as uniquely fragile: without deliberate preservation, digital memory disappears far faster than paper.

## Details

- **Two branches:** Material digitized from physical objects loses the original but keeps a copy, while born-digital material (the web, research data, email archives) exists only digitally, and vanishes fastest of all.
- **Threats:** Media and hardware obsolescence, file-format obsolescence, bit rot, and link rot. The useful lifetime of a storage medium or a format is measured in years, not centuries, so preservation must be active and repeated, not passive storage.
- **Strategies:** Migration of content into current formats, emulation of obsolete environments, and redundant preservation infrastructure. In scholarly publishing LOCKSS and CLOCKSS play exactly this role; at the cultural scale, the Internet Archive and national web archives do the same for the public web.
- **Open science tie:** Scholarly publications and research data are themselves digital heritage. Preservation infrastructure, repository practice, and versioning are what keep the open record alive once the originally published copy has moved or disappeared.

> [!NOTE]
> Preservation and open access interlock: an openly licensed object nobody can still render is not accessible, and open repositories without archival arrangements risk silent loss.
